\section{Profile}
I take models to production and I report what they actually do. On the traditional side that means
gradient-boosted and global forecasting models measured out-of-sample against a mandatory
seasonal-naive baseline, with conformal intervals derived from residuals and an accuracy
certification that says plainly which forecasts clear the client's tolerance and which do not. On the
generative side it means agents that call tools through declared schemas, retrieval that runs before
inference with authorisation applied inside the retriever, and guardrails that mark any number the
model did not get from a tool. Software Developer at CortexCraft.ai, in Python and Java.

\section{Selected Projects}

\textbf{Demand Forecasting System} \textit{Python, PyCaret, scikit-learn, Docker, Streamlit, Playwright}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Sole author. Monthly forecasts across roughly 1,500 product series of 36 points each, from a
        tiered model zoo of about 150 labelled configurations: pure-numpy statistical models, a
        global ML tier, and a PyCaret sweep on aggregates.
  \item The ML tier fits one model across all series pooled rather than one per series, which is
        what makes a 48-configuration sweep affordable on 36-point histories, and forecasts the
        12-month horizon directly rather than recursively, so no prediction is ever fed back as an
        input and error cannot compound across the horizon.
  \item Scored out-of-sample on WAPE and MASE rather than MAPE, which explodes on zero months, with
        seasonal-naive as a mandatory baseline that ships if no candidate beats it. Prediction
        intervals are conformal, computed from model residuals rather than from the variance of the
        data, and a GREEN / AMBER / RED certification reports that median product-level WAPE is
        16.6\% and 18.7\% of series land inside the client's requested 10\% band, rather than
        narrowing every interval until the requirement appears to be met.
\end{itemize}
